# Worked example: from a lorry marketplace to "Yakin"

A real student team's board, simplified, with no personal data. Use it to show what honest pivots look like. The rendered version is `assets/example-board.json`.

## The starting idea (solution-first, which is the classic mistake)

"A Lalamove for business lorries: companies post loads, transporters bid." Desk research showed a crowded market, and that marketplaces get bypassed after the first match. That research was preparation only, not evidence about customers. The team went to the board and started from customers instead.

## Experiment 1: frozen-food businesses with sudden orders

- **Customer:** frozen-food and halal poultry businesses that get sudden orders but have no logistics.
- **Problem:** "Sudden orders we can't refuse but can't deliver."
- **Riskiest assumption:** they lose sales today because they can't find transport fast.
- **Method:** phone calls plus street interviews.
- **Result:**
  - The calls went unanswered, so that part is **untested**, not failed.
  - In street interviews with food businesses, suppliers said they deliver themselves.
  - The pain that came up unprompted was different: *when the halal chicken supplier runs out, the restaurant drops chicken dishes or closes, because it can't trust an unknown supplier.*
- **Decision:** **Pivot, on both customer and problem.**
  - The team first wrote "Persevere", but the customer and problem had both changed, so it was a pivot.
  - The new pain was heard from 2 of 2 relevant interviews. That is a signal worth testing, not proof.
- **Learning:** the real pain sat with restaurants' supply, not with transport.

## Experiment 2: halal restaurants that rely on one chicken supplier

- **Customer:** halal restaurants and cafés in one town that buy chicken from a single supplier.
- **Problem:** "When my supplier runs out, I take chicken off the menu or close, because I can't trust a new supplier."
- **Solution (unlocked after the pivot signal, so it could be shown):** a clickable prototype listing nearby suppliers with verified halal status, prices, minimum orders and a quote request.
- **Riskiest assumption:** owners will trust *our* verification enough to act on it.
  - The second risk was parked for later: stockouts might be market-wide, leaving no backup with stock.
- **Method:** in-person prototype demo with a no-money sign-up (contact details for the pilot).
- **Criterion (locked before going out):** at least 3 owners sign up, out of a planned 8 visits.
- **Result:** 5 restaurants visited. 3 confirmed the problem, tried the prototype and gave contact details. The other 2 did not.
- **Decision:** **Persevere.**
  - The criterion's count (3 sign-ups) was met. The behaviour, giving contact details, is strong evidence.
  - Caveat: only 5 visits, and nobody paid, by the workshop rule.
- **Learning:** the restaurant side acts. The next risk is who pays.

## Experiment 3 (planned): the supplier side

- **Customer:** chicken suppliers and wholesalers in the same town.
- **Riskiest assumption:** suppliers will pay for each verified restaurant lead.
- **Method:** pre-sell with a letter of intent, no money collected.
- **Criterion:** set by the team before outreach.

## What this example teaches

1. Starting from a solution wastes time; the board pulled the team back to customer and problem.
2. The best pivots come from what customers say *unprompted*.
3. An unanswered call is untested, not a "no".
4. Name the decision honestly: changing the customer means a Pivot, even when it feels like progress.
5. After the problem is confirmed, the riskiest assumption moves, here from "do they care?" to "who pays?"
